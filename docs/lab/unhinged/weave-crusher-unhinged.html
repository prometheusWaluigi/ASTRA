The idea in one breath

Weaving interlaces materials so no single strand carries the load alone. Build a robot's body as a 3D woven shell and you get a paradox: stiff along its length (it can haul 70× its own weight) but soft in bending (it can flex to walk) — and when you crush it, damage spreads through the weave instead of snapping one fatal part. Resilience is in the grammar of the fabric, not bolted on after.

How it works

  • Woven corners. The whole robot assembles from one modular unit — a 3D woven corner — like bricks made of cloth.
  • Eigenvalue tuning. The designers compute the structure's vibration modes to find which shapes bear load and which shapes actuate, then weave toward both.
  • Damage tolerance. Extreme compression breaks individual strands, but the interlaced network reroutes stress around the breaks. Performance stays repeatable.

Why it's unhinged

Every other robot paradigm treats damage as failure. This one treats the weave like kintsugi: the gold seams in this demo mark strands that broke and were repaired — and the robot walks exactly as well with gold in it. The stomp isn't destruction; it's the experiment. Press hard. Count the gold.

Paper: Tu & Filipov, Active 3D weaves for load-bearing and damage-resilient locomotion, arXiv 2609.32099 · caught by the lab's paper hunt (textile weaving computation). This piece is an explanatory sketch, not a result — the numbers (70×, repeatable post-crush gait) are the paper's.
